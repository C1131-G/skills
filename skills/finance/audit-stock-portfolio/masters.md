# The three masters as checklists

Run each stock through all three. A stock that fails Buffett's business test is not rescued by the other two.

## Warren Buffett — a wonderful business at a fair price

1. **Circle of competence.** Can the investor explain in two sentences how the company earns money? If not, it is outside the circle.
2. **Durable moat.** Name the moat: brand, network effect, switching cost, cost advantage, licence or regulation. Evidence it lasts: stable or rising gross margins and ROCE over 10 years.
3. **Economics.** ROE above 15% for most of 10 years **without** high debt; growing owner earnings (operating cash flow − maintenance capex).
4. **Management.** Honest and capable: allocates capital well, avoids unrelated diversification, no aggressive accounting, candid annual-report letters.
5. **Price.** Intrinsic value from owner earnings or a conservative DCF; buy only below it with a margin of safety (≥25% for steady compounders, more for cyclicals).
6. **Holding period.** "If you aren't willing to own a stock for ten years, don't think about owning it for ten minutes."

## Charlie Munger — avoid stupidity, then sit

1. **Invert.** Write the three most likely ways this investment loses half its value. Check each against the evidence.
2. **Incentives.** How are promoters and management paid and rewarded? Related-party transactions, a sister company that benefits, or rising promoter salaries while profits fall are red flags.
3. **Quality over cheapness.** Prefer a great business at a fair price to a fair business at a great price.
4. **Few, big, understood bets.** Concentration is fine only in businesses the investor truly understands — and never with leverage.
5. **Sit.** Don't trade. Turnover adds tax, cost and mistakes. A Hold verdict is a valid, often the best, outcome.
6. **Psychology check.** Is the thesis driven by social proof, recent price rise, or a story the investor wants to believe?

## Anand Srinivasan — moats in ordinary businesses

He writes The Value Observer (moatinvesting.substack.com) and wrote *The Investor: Ordinary Stocks, Extraordinary Profits*. Apply the theme those titles state, and read his posts on the specific company or sector when available rather than assuming his view:

1. **Ordinary can compound.** Boring, unglamorous businesses with a real moat often beat exciting stories. Don't penalise a stock for being dull.
2. **Find the moat first, then the price.** Same order as Buffett — business quality, then valuation.
3. **Indian context.** Check the business against Indian realities: promoter behaviour, government policy and PSU risk, rural vs urban demand, competition from unorganised players.

Never attribute a specific stock pick or quote to any of the three without a source you have read in this session.
